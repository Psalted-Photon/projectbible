Everything the app does, in plain language. Free, offline-first, and private: your reading, notes, and highlights live on your device, and syncing to an account is optional.

1.1 The reading page

  • Continuous scrolling — chapters load as you scroll, so you can read from Genesis 1 straight through without tapping "next chapter". Scrolling backwards loads the previous chapter the same way.
  • Verse layout — three ways to see the text:
    • Each verse on its own line, the classic study layout.
    • Flowing paragraphs with small verse numbers, closest to a printed Bible.
    • Flowing paragraphs with no verse numbers at all, for distraction-free reading.
  • Word wrap — on by default. Turning it off lets long lines run off the side instead of wrapping, which suits poetry and interlinear rows.
  • Your place is remembered — the app reopens to the translation, book, and chapter you were last reading, even after closing it completely.

1.2 What appears in the text

  • Words of Jesus in red — red-letter text, shown correctly in dark, light, and sepia themes. Can be turned off.
  • Section headings — the small pericope titles between verses ("The Sermon on the Mount"). Can be turned off.
  • Footnotes — small blue numbers in the text. Tap one to read the translator's note.
  • Cross-reference markers — same small numbers, in grey rather than blue, when the note points to another passage instead of explaining wording. The app tells the two apart automatically.
  • Bold and italic — preserved from translations that use them, including the italics some Bibles use for supplied words.
  • Art icons — a small icon next to verses that a painting depicts. Tap it to open the artwork. Can be turned off.
  • Place-name underlines — an optional faint dotted underline under multi-word place names like "Red Sea" or "Abel Beth Maacah", so you can see which words act as a single place when tapped. Off by default, and needs the encyclopedia pack.
  • Themed titles — book and chapter titles carry a subtle colored shadow matching the book's category. Can be turned off.

1.3 Book introductions

Every book opens with an introduction panel covering who wrote it, when, and what it is about. It can be opened at any time from the book title, and closing it returns you to exactly where you were reading.

2.1 The book and chapter picker

  • All 66 books, grouped and color-coded into ten categories — Pentateuch, Historical, Wisdom, Major Prophets, Minor Prophets, Gospels, Acts, Pauline Epistles, General Epistles, and Eschaton — so you can find a book by shape and color rather than by reading every name.
  • Chapter grid — pick any chapter directly, no scrolling through a long list.
  • Books you can't open are hidden — if the translation you're in is New Testament only, the Old Testament books don't appear.

2.2 Moving between passages

  • Back button — steps back through the passages you've visited, like a browser's back button. It only appears when there's somewhere to go back to.
  • Tap any reference to follow it — references inside cross-references, commentaries, and encyclopedia articles are all live links. The verse you land on flashes briefly in its book's category color so you can spot it immediately.
  • Reference shorthand is understood — "Ex 20:21", "Am 5:18-20", or a bare "3:14" while you're already in a book all resolve correctly. Common abbreviations and alternate book spellings are recognised.
  • The toolbar follows your scrolling — as you scroll into a new chapter, the book and chapter shown in the toolbar update to match, without yanking the page around.

  • Translation picker — switch between every translation you have installed from the toolbar.
  • Switching keeps your place — you stay on the same book and chapter. If that book doesn't exist in the translation you picked (switching to a Greek New Testament while in Genesis, say), the app moves you to the nearest book that does.
  • Daily drivers — set a preferred translation for each combination of language and testament: English Old Testament, English New Testament, Hebrew Old Testament, Greek Old Testament, Greek New Testament, and Hebrew New Testament. Cross-references, search, and other features then open in the right translation for the passage automatically, rather than always defaulting to one.

4.3 The layers

  • English gloss — the English equivalent under each word. Always on; it's the point of an interlinear.
  • Transliteration — how the word is pronounced, in English letters.
  • Lemma — the dictionary form of the word, as you'd look it up.
  • Strong's number — the standard reference number for the word.
  • Parsing — the grammar: part of speech, tense, case, gender, number.

Tap any word in the text to open a full study panel for it. Works on English words and on Greek and Hebrew words.

5.1 For Greek and Hebrew words

  • Entry information — the word, its transliteration, its dictionary form, and its Strong's number.
  • Short definition — the quick answer.
  • Full definition — the complete lexicon entry.
  • KJV usage — every way the King James Version rendered this word, which shows its range of meaning at a glance.
  • Derivation — the root the word comes from.
  • Inflection forms — the other forms this word takes across the text.
  • Occurrences — every verse in the Bible using this word, so you can see how it's actually used rather than only how it's defined.
  • Related words — synonyms and words sharing a root.

5.2 For English words

  • Dictionary definitions — grouped by part of speech.
  • Plurals and variants are handled — tapping "waters" finds "water"; tapping a verb form finds its base form.
  • People are recognised — tapping a name opens that person's entry with the verses they appear in, rather than a dictionary definition of the word.

5.3 Grammar in plain English

Morphology codes are translated out of shorthand. Instead of V-PAI-3S you read "Verb, Present, Active, Indicative, Third person, Singular", and instead of HNcmsa, "Hebrew, Noun, common, masculine, singular, absolute". All three coding systems the packs use are covered — Greek, Hebrew/Aramaic, and the STEPBible part-of-speech format.

Full articles from the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia — a complete, public-domain Bible encyclopedia, included free and available offline.

6.1 Opening an article

Select a word or phrase in the text and choose More Info. The app works out whether you tapped a place, a person, or a general subject and opens the matching article.

6.2 What an article shows

  • Overview — the summary: what kind of thing it is, its other names, and where it appears.
  • Article — the full encyclopedia text, broken into a collapsible table of contents. Long entries stay navigable instead of becoming a wall of text, and repeated section headings are handled so the contents list stays clean.
  • Verses — every verse mentioning the subject, grouped by book in Bible order, each one tappable.
  • Map — for places, a pin at the location, when coordinates are known.

6.3 Linked to the dictionary

Where an encyclopedia entry and a dictionary entry describe the same thing, the app connects them, so you can move between the short definition and the long article without searching again.

6.4 Place-name underlines

The encyclopedia pack also powers the optional dotted underlines described in 1.2, which mark multi-word place names so you can see what's tappable as a unit.

  • Strong's — original-language entries

  • Journal — your journal entries
  • Saved verses

Typing a Strong's number straight into the box (G26, H430, g0026) jumps directly to that entry.

Results are grouped in a tree you can collapse, and each category is capped so one huge category can't bury the rest. Commentary search is heavy, so it runs only when you press Enter rather than on every keystroke.

9.2 Advanced search

For precise work, with built-in help on every option:

  • Match type — contains anywhere, starts with, ends with, whole word only, word begins with, word ends with.
  • Must also contain — narrow results to verses that include another term too.
  • Must NOT contain — exclude verses containing a term.
  • Proximity — find words near each other, within a distance you set.
  • Include plurals — match "waters" when you searched "water".
  • Include synonyms — widen to related words.
  • Case-insensitive — on or off.
  • Show pronunciation — display transliterations alongside original-language results.

Every one of these has a ? button explaining what it does with worked examples.

  • Pause and resume.
  • Jump to a verse and start reading from there.
  • Speed control from 0.8× to 1.5×.
  • Auto-advance — keep reading into the next chapter automatically, or stop at the end of this one.

16.3 Following along

Two independent effects — use either, both, or neither:

  • Highlight the verse being read — tints the current verse. On by default.
  • Drifting glow — a soft cloud of light that glides along the words as they're spoken. Off by default.

The glow is deliberately soft and a few words wide. The speech model hands back finished audio without telling the app exactly when each word is spoken, so the pace is an estimate. A sharp highlight on the wrong word looks broken; a blurred cloud is almost always covering the right word somewhere in its span, and reads as atmosphere rather than error.

16.4 Section headings

18.1 Opening a window

Drag inward from any edge of the screen — top, left, right, or bottom — and a new window opens at the size you dragged to. Drag further for a bigger window.

The gesture is deliberately careful about not firing by accident: it waits until it can tell which direction you're actually moving before committing, and the bottom edge leaves a gap in the middle for the Android home gesture, so you don't open a window every time you try to leave the app.

18.2 What can go in a window

Bible text · Map · Notes · Word study · Commentaries · Journal · Art

Up to six windows at once. Each is resizable, and each remembers its own state — a second Bible window can sit on a different chapter from the main one.

18.3 Panes

A second, simpler docking system for app-level surfaces: Settings, Map, Packs, Search, Notes, and Commentaries. These open left, right, or bottom. On a phone they open wider (75% of the screen) than on a larger display (40%), since a narrow pane on a phone is unusable.

18.4 Rotation

The app is locked to portrait by default. Allowing rotation is a setting; when the screen turns, the app fades through the change rather than snapping.

20.1 Account

Create an account with an email and password. You can also change your name, change your password, reset a forgotten password, and delete your account outright.

20.2 What syncs

Signing in syncs your personal data across devices:

  • Notes
  • Verse highlights and word highlights
  • Saved verses
  • Journal entries
  • Reading plans
  • Reading progress

Plus a subset of settings that should follow you: theme, timezone, your default translations, interlinear preferences, red-letter, section headings, art icons, and themed titles.

20.3 What deliberately doesn't sync

Font size, line spacing, verse layout, word wrap, screen rotation, and update checks stay on each device. A phone and a desktop rarely want the same font size.

20.4 Working offline

  • Changes made offline are queued and uploaded when you're back online. Nothing is lost.
  • Repeated edits to the same thing are collapsed into one upload rather than replayed one by one.
  • Failed uploads retry with increasing delays, up to fifteen minutes apart, so a flaky connection doesn't hammer the server.
  • Live updates — with two devices open at once, a change on one appears on the other without a refresh.
  • Reading progress merges rather than overwrites. A chapter ticked on your phone and a different chapter ticked on your tablet both survive; neither device wins by being last.
  • A fresh install won't wipe your account. Settings only come down when the server's copy is genuinely newer than what this device last synced, so signing in on a new phone never overwrites your real settings with an empty one.

16.6 Drifting glow

src/lib/ttsGlow.ts. Two design decisions worth preserving:

  • Why soft and wide (:5-10) — the standard Piper export returns finished audio and keeps its internal per-word durations to itself, so pacing is an estimate.
